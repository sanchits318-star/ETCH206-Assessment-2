CONTEMPORARY THEORIES · AUSTRALIAN POLICY

COMPONENT 01

Foundations of infant and toddler practice

Australian infant and toddler education recognises children from birth as capable, active participants in their own learning. Practice brings together developmental knowledge, secure relationships, cultural responsiveness and the national frameworks that guide quality early childhood education and care.

✿
THEORETICAL LENSES

Three complementary traditions

These perspectives help educators interpret how infants and toddlers learn through relationships, exploration and respectful care.

🗣️

Vygotsky · Sociocultural theory

Learning is socially and culturally mediated. Through shared attention, conversation, modelling and responsive scaffolding, educators extend what a child can explore independently.

Shared meaning-making
♡

Bowlby & Ainsworth · Attachment theory

Consistent, emotionally responsive relationships provide a secure base for exploration and a safe haven when children need comfort. Trust supports participation and learning.

Secure relationships
🌿

Gerber & Pikler · Pedagogical respect

Respectful care values unhurried observation, freedom of movement and bodily autonomy. Educators communicate before acting and respond to each infant’s cues.

Agency & autonomy
AUSTRALIAN FRAMEWORKS

EYLF V2.0 and the National Quality Standard

Belonging, Being and Becoming

The Early Years Learning Framework for Australia V2.0 (AGDE, 2022) guides curriculum decision-making from birth. It positions children as active learners and emphasises relationships, play, intentionality, inclusion, sustainability and cultural responsiveness.

The framework brings together eight principles, seven practices and five learning outcomes. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives are integral to meaningful curriculum and respectful partnerships.

FIVE EYLF LEARNING OUTCOMES
  1. Children have a strong sense of identity.
  2. Children are connected with and contribute to their world.
  3. Children have a strong sense of wellbeing.
  4. Children are confident and involved learners.
  5. Children are effective communicators.
CHILDREN’S RIGHTS

Respecting every child’s voice

The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child provides an important rights-based foundation for early childhood practice.

Article 3

The child’s best interests are a primary consideration.

Article 6

Every child has the right to life, survival and development.

Article 12

Children have the right to express views and have them considered, including through gaze, gesture and other pre-verbal cues.

Article 30

Children have the right to enjoy their culture, language and community.

Article 31

Children have the right to rest, leisure and play.

What this means in practice

Notice each child’s cues, protect time for uninterrupted exploration, build genuine partnerships with families, and make everyday decisions through the lens of safety, dignity, inclusion and belonging.
